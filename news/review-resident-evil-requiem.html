Resident Evil Requiem Review: Ambitious, Terrifying, and Far From Perfect

Capcom's latest Resident Evil tries to bring survival horror and action together through two very different protagonists. The result is ambitious, polished, occasionally terrifying, and far from flawless.

ARIYA REVIEWS • THE GAME TIMES
THE GAME TIMES REVIEW
9.5
EXCELLENT

A DAMN GOOD RESIDENT EVIL GAME

Ambitious, terrifying and ridiculously polished — but not flawless.

“Capcom really said: horror fans and action fans are fighting? Fine. Have both.” — Ariya

The Verdict

Resident Evil Requiem is one of Capcom’s strongest Resident Evil games in years.

But calling it perfect would be bullshit.

It is ambitious, gorgeous, occasionally terrifying, and ridiculously polished. At the same time, it can feel like two completely different Resident Evil games fighting for control of the same campaign.

And strangely enough, that is also why it works.

Requiem does not try to choose between survival horror and action. Instead, it puts both philosophies into the same experience through two very different protagonists.

Grace Ashcroft represents vulnerability.

Leon S. Kennedy represents power.

The result is uneven in places, but when it clicks, Requiem feels like Capcom confidently embracing everything that has made Resident Evil what it is.

Grace Ashcroft Is the Heart of Requiem

Grace may be the most important new character Resident Evil has introduced in years.

She is not Leon.

She is not Jill.

She is not Chris.

And she is certainly not another walking action movie who enters a room and immediately starts dismantling B.O.W.s.

Grace is an FBI analyst who is genuinely afraid.

That distinction is incredibly important.

Capcom understands something that horror games sometimes forget: fear works better when the character is afraid too.

Grace's sections lean heavily into survival horror. Resources are limited. Environments are oppressive. Exploration matters. Encounters can make running away feel considerably smarter than fighting.

The first-person perspective makes everything even more intimate.

You are not watching Grace get chased.

You are being chased with her.

That distinction changes everything.

The Rhodes Hill Care Center is one of the game's strongest environments, combining exploration, puzzles, environmental storytelling, and oppressive atmosphere in a way that recalls some of the franchise's best locations.

This is the kind of Resident Evil where you hear something behind you, stop moving, and genuinely do not want to turn around.

That is where Requiem is at its best.

Then Leon Shows Up

And suddenly, the game goes:

“Enough hiding. Give the man a shotgun.”

Leon is almost the complete opposite of Grace.

Where Grace is about surviving, Leon is about fighting back.

His sections lean harder into third-person action, heavier weapons, aggressive encounters, and the swagger that has become synonymous with his character.

This is where Requiem channels the DNA of Resident Evil 4, and honestly, it works.

The dual-protagonist structure allows Capcom to explore both sides of Resident Evil without forcing one character to carry the entire experience.

Grace represents vulnerability.

Leon represents power.

It is a clever contrast.

But there is a problem.

The Balance Isn't Perfect

Requiem initially establishes an excellent balance between horror and action.

Then Leon's side gradually becomes more dominant.

That is where some of the game's horror identity begins to slip.

Grace's early sections establish genuine vulnerability. You feel under-equipped. You feel exposed. You question whether an encounter is even worth fighting.

Then Leon comes crashing through the door with a shotgun and suddenly everything feels different.

It is fun.

It is badass.

But it also removes some of the helplessness that made Grace's sections so effective.

And that is probably the biggest criticism of Requiem:

Sometimes it forgets that being powerless is part of what makes Resident Evil scary.

The action is rarely bad. In fact, much of it is excellent.

The issue is emotional consistency.

When the game teaches you to fear what is around the corner, then gives you enough firepower to confidently charge toward it, the tension naturally changes.

The Horror

When Requiem wants to scare you, it absolutely can.

The atmosphere is phenomenal.

Lighting, sound design, creature design, environmental detail, and level design come together to create an experience that can be genuinely uncomfortable.

More importantly, Capcom is not relying entirely on cheap jumpscares.

Some of the strongest horror comes from anticipation.

You know something is there.

You do not know where.

You do not know when it will appear.

And you do not have enough ammunition to feel comfortable.

That is Resident Evil.

The game understands that sometimes the scariest thing it can do is simply make you wait.

The Combat

Capcom has essentially taken lessons from Resident Evil 2 Remake, Resident Evil 4 Remake, and Resident Evil Village and brought elements of all three philosophies into Requiem.

The gunplay feels responsive.

Melee attacks have weight.

Enemies react convincingly to damage.

And Leon's encounters can descend into absolute chaos.

But this is also where Requiem occasionally loses some of its survival-horror identity.

Once you are properly equipped, certain encounters stop feeling like:

“How am I going to survive this?”

and start feeling like:

“Okay. How many bullets does this thing need?”

That is not inherently bad.

It is simply a completely different emotional experience.

And Requiem is at its best when it knows exactly which emotion it wants you to feel.

Exploration and Puzzles

This is another area where Requiem understands the assignment.

The strongest environments encourage you to actually look around.

You search rooms.

Find resources.

Connect clues.

Backtrack.

Solve puzzles.

Discover environmental storytelling.

You are not simply being pushed from one cutscene to another.

That matters because Resident Evil works best when the environment itself becomes part of the gameplay.

The best locations in Requiem understand this.

A locked door is not just an obstacle.

A strange object is not just decoration.

A seemingly insignificant room can contain the clue you need twenty minutes later.

It is the kind of design that rewards curiosity rather than simply demanding forward momentum.

Modern horror games could learn a thing or two from it.

The Bosses

This is where Requiem is less impressive.

There are memorable encounters, but not every boss reaches the same standard as the rest of the game.

Some fights simply do not have the impact you would expect after the buildup surrounding them.

That is frustrating because Requiem can create incredible atmosphere.

Then, occasionally, it throws you into a boss arena and leaves you thinking:

“That's it?”

The game needed a few more encounters that genuinely felt like events.

Not necessarily longer fights.

Not necessarily harder fights.

Just encounters that matched the atmosphere and anticipation the game had spent so much time building.

The Story

Without getting into spoilers, Requiem is heavily invested in Resident Evil's history.

It knows you are a longtime fan.

It knows you remember Raccoon City.

It knows what Leon means to this franchise.

And it absolutely uses that history.

Sometimes brilliantly.

Sometimes a little too much.

The strongest moments are the ones where the mythology actually serves the characters.

The weaker moments feel more like Capcom turning toward the audience and saying:

“Remember this?”

That fan service can work when it has emotional weight behind it.

When it does not, it risks feeling like a reference for the sake of a reference.

The final stretch also does not maintain the same strength as some of the earlier sections.

The beginning feels focused.

The middle becomes ambitious.

The ending gets a little messy.

The Graphics

This might be the most visually impressive Resident Evil yet.

The RE Engine continues to be an absolute monster.

Character faces.

Skin.

Hair.

Rain.

Lighting.

Reflections.

Environments.

Creature detail.

Everything is ridiculously detailed.

The visual presentation is one of Requiem's biggest strengths, particularly during its atmospheric environments where lighting and environmental detail work together to sell the horror.

On the technical side, however, that visual ambition comes at a cost.

Ray tracing can be extremely demanding, particularly on systems with lower VRAM.

So yes, Requiem can look absolutely insane.

Just do not expect an aging gaming PC to casually run everything at maximum settings.

The Length

Requiem is not a 20-hour Resident Evil.

A focused playthrough can be completed in roughly 9–10 hours, while players who explore more thoroughly and chase secrets and completion goals can spend considerably longer with it.

And honestly?

That is not necessarily a problem.

Resident Evil has always benefited from replayability.

The real question is not:

“Is it long enough?”

It is:

“Is it worth replaying?”

Requiem gives players reasons to return through its different characters, difficulty options, exploration, secrets, and progression.

I would rather have a tightly paced 10-hour horror game than a bloated 25-hour campaign padded with meaningless content.

Where Does Requiem Rank?

This is where things get interesting.

Resident Evil 2 Remake remains one of Capcom's strongest modern survival-horror experiences.

Resident Evil 4 Remake remains the benchmark for action-horror.

Resident Evil 7 brought genuine fear back to the franchise.

Resident Evil Village delivered scale and spectacle.

Requiem does not completely replace any of them.

Instead, it tries to combine them.

And somehow, most of the time, it works.

That is perhaps Requiem's greatest achievement.

It does not completely abandon the franchise's survival-horror roots in favor of action.

It also does not pretend the action-heavy evolution of Resident Evil never happened.

It tries to have both.

Ariya's Final Take

Resident Evil Requiem is not perfect.

The pacing becomes uneven.

The action eventually takes over too much.

Some boss fights do not hit hard enough.

The story loses some momentum toward the end.

And honestly?

I wanted more Grace.

But here is the thing.

I would rather have Capcom swing this hard and occasionally miss than play it safe.

Because when Requiem is at its best, this is Resident Evil firing on every cylinder.

Grace gives us fear.

Leon gives us chaos.

The environments give us atmosphere.

The combat gives us adrenaline.

And the franchise's history gives everything an emotional weight that a completely new horror game simply could not replicate.

Capcom did not make another Resident Evil 4.

It did not make another Resident Evil 7.

It made something that tries to sit somewhere between them.

And that is exactly why I respect it.

Resident Evil Requiem is messy in places.

But it is ambitious.

It is terrifying.

It is ridiculously polished.

And most importantly, it still understands what makes Resident Evil special.

THE GAME TIMES SCORE: 9.5/10

A damn good Resident Evil game — just not a flawless one.

And honestly?

After all these years, Capcom still knows how to make us afraid of the dark.

9.5
