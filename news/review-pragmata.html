PRAGMATA Review: Capcom's Weirdest Risk Might Be One of Its Best

Capcom's bizarre lunar adventure is stylish, ambitious, mechanically inventive and surprisingly emotional.

AUGUST 2026 • THE GAME TIMES REVIEWS

The Verdict

I genuinely did not expect PRAGMATA to become one of my favorite games of 2026.

Not because I thought it looked bad.

Quite the opposite.

After years of watching Capcom tease this bizarre science-fiction project, the bigger question was whether the finished game could actually live up to the promise of those early reveals.

It did.

PRAGMATA is weird.

It is stylish.

It is emotional.

It is mechanically ambitious.

And, perhaps most importantly, it feels like somebody actually wanted to make a video game rather than another product designed around an industry trend.

This is a linear, single-player sci-fi action game arriving at a time when AAA gaming is increasingly obsessed with enormous open worlds, endless progression systems and live-service models.

PRAGMATA basically walks into 2026 and asks:

“What if we just made a really good game?”

Honestly?

Thank you, Capcom.

A Beautiful Lunar Nightmare

PRAGMATA takes place on a lunar research facility where astronaut Hugh Williams becomes stranded following a catastrophic incident.

There, he encounters Diana, an android girl with extraordinary abilities.

Together, they must survive a facility overrun by hostile machines while uncovering the truth behind what happened.

On paper, the premise sounds familiar.

The execution is anything but.

The lunar colony is one of the game's greatest strengths.

It is futuristic without feeling sterile. Bright neon signage sits alongside industrial machinery, while beautiful futuristic architecture exists beside abandoned laboratories and decaying facilities.

There is a constant sense that this place was once alive.

Now something has gone horribly wrong.

The visual identity is fantastic.

PRAGMATA looks like a lost futuristic game from an alternate timeline where classic science fiction, Japanese action games and cinematic sci-fi were thrown into the same room.

And somehow, it works.

The Combat Is Where PRAGMATA Becomes Special

Let's get straight to it.

PRAGMATA's combat is fucking brilliant.

At first glance, it looks like another third-person shooter.

Shoot robots.

Take cover.

Move forward.

Repeat.

Except Diana hacks the enemies while Hugh shoots them.

And this is not some annoying companion gimmick bolted onto a shooting system.

It is the foundation of the entire combat experience.

Diana can manipulate enemy defenses through a real-time hacking system, allowing Hugh to expose weaknesses before dealing serious damage.

That means you are constantly making decisions.

Hack.

Shoot.

Move.

Reload.

Hack again.

Change weapons.

Prioritize targets.

Manage resources.

All while giant murderous machines are trying to turn you into astronaut paste.

The genius is that PRAGMATA does not simply add a hacking mechanic to a shooter.

It makes hacking part of the shooting.

That distinction is everything.

The Hacking System

This could have been terrible.

It could have completely interrupted the action.

It could have become one of those mechanics you tolerate because the developers desperately wanted their game to be different.

Instead, it becomes addictive.

You start recognizing enemy patterns.

You learn which targets need to be hacked first.

You become faster at manipulating the system.

Eventually, your brain starts switching between hacking and shooting almost automatically.

And when everything clicks?

It feels fucking fantastic.

There is a rhythm to it.

You hack an enemy.

Expose its weakness.

Unload your weapon.

Move to the next target.

Suddenly, what initially looked like two separate mechanics becomes one fluid combat loop.

That is probably the biggest compliment I can give PRAGMATA:

I don't want Capcom to abandon this mechanic.

I want them to make it even deeper in whatever comes next.

Hugh and Diana

And then there is the part I absolutely was not prepared for.

The relationship.

Hugh could have easily been another generic protagonist.

The grizzled astronaut.

The reluctant hero.

The guy who says three words per cutscene.

Instead, his relationship with Diana gradually becomes the emotional center of the game.

Diana is curious.

She is intelligent.

She is funny.

And she has a sense of wonder that contrasts beautifully with Hugh's more grounded personality.

At its core, PRAGMATA is a human-and-android relationship story wrapped inside a science-fiction action game.

And it works because the game actually gives them time together.

It does not constantly bombard you with exposition explaining how much they care about one another.

You simply watch the relationship develop.

That is far more effective.

The quieter moments matter because the game allows the characters to breathe.

And yeah...

PRAGMATA got me.

I came for the guns.

I stayed for these two idiots trying to survive the moon together.

The Story

PRAGMATA is not reinventing science-fiction storytelling.

It uses familiar ideas.

Artificial intelligence.

Corporate greed.

Humanity versus technology.

Identity.

Family.

Sacrifice.

But execution matters more than originality alone.

The story does not constantly scream at you about how deep it is.

Instead, it allows the characters to carry much of the emotional weight.

And when PRAGMATA gets quiet, that is when it becomes most effective.

There are moments where the action disappears and you are simply watching Hugh and Diana interact.

Those moments are essential.

Without them, the combat would not carry the same emotional weight.

The story works because the relationship gives the player's actions meaning beyond simply surviving the next encounter.

An Art Direction With an Identity

PRAGMATA is gorgeous.

But there is an important distinction between being technically impressive and being visually distinctive.

PRAGMATA is both.

The character designs are immediately recognizable.

Diana's design is particularly memorable.

The lunar colony feels like an actual place rather than a collection of corridors built purely to move the player from one fight to another.

The machines look threatening without becoming visually generic.

And the game constantly finds interesting ways to combine bright futuristic technology with dark, abandoned environments.

Most importantly, PRAGMATA has an identity.

You can look at a screenshot and immediately know what game you are looking at.

That is increasingly rare in AAA gaming.

Sound and Music

The audio design deserves more credit too.

Weapons have satisfying impact.

Machines sound appropriately mechanical and threatening.

Environmental audio helps sell the isolation of the lunar facility.

And the soundtrack understands when to step forward and when to disappear.

That restraint is particularly effective during the quieter Hugh-and-Diana moments.

The game understands something simple:

Sometimes silence hits harder than an orchestra.

Level Design: Thank God It's Linear

Here is something I did not expect to appreciate this much.

PRAGMATA is linear.

And I fucking love it.

There is no enormous map.

No sea of icons.

No checklist disguised as content.

No pointless crafting system designed primarily to stretch the runtime.

You move through carefully constructed environments.

You fight.

Explore.

Solve problems.

Upgrade.

Move forward.

And the pacing benefits enormously from it.

PRAGMATA understands something the industry occasionally forgets:

Linear does not mean limited.

It means focused.

Every environment has a purpose.

Every encounter contributes to the pacing.

And because the game is not constantly trying to distract you with side activities, the relationship between Hugh and Diana remains at the center of the experience.

The Enemies

The enemy variety is another surprisingly strong aspect.

The machines are not simply bullet sponges.

Different enemies require different approaches.

And because of the hacking system, understanding their defenses becomes part of the strategy.

The game constantly asks:

“What's the smartest way to kill this thing?”

rather than:

“How much ammunition do you have?”

That is a subtle but important difference.

PRAGMATA wants you to understand its combat system rather than simply overpower it.

The better you become at combining hacking with weapon usage, the more satisfying encounters become.

The Boss Fights

This is one area where I would not call PRAGMATA perfect.

Some encounters are fantastic.

Others do not quite reach the same level as the game's best combat sequences.

That is disappointing considering how strong the core combat system is.

Still, the overall gameplay remains strong enough that even when an encounter is not spectacular, it is usually still fun to play.

And that matters.

A mediocre boss attached to an excellent combat system is considerably easier to forgive than a spectacular boss attached to terrible gameplay.

The Length

PRAGMATA is not enormous.

And again...

Thank fucking God.

The main story sits comfortably within the sort of length that allows the game to introduce its mechanics, develop its characters, escalate its story and finish before the formula becomes exhausting.

Would I have played another five hours?

Absolutely.

But I am also glad Capcom did not turn PRAGMATA into a 30-hour open-world RPG simply because modern AAA games are expected to be enormous.

PRAGMATA knows what it wants to be.

And it leaves before overstaying its welcome.

That restraint is one of its greatest strengths.

What Doesn't Work?

Okay.

We need to be honest.

Because this is not a fanboy review.

PRAGMATA has weaknesses.

The story occasionally leans heavily on familiar science-fiction ideas.

Some encounters can become mechanically overwhelming.

The hacking system may become repetitive for certain players.

And the narrative does not always reach the heights its premise initially suggests.

Those criticisms are fair.

I do not disagree with them.

I just...

do not care that much.

Because the gameplay is that good.

The characters are that charming.

The world is that beautiful.

And the overall experience is so confident in what it wants to be that its imperfections never managed to kill my enthusiasm.

PRAGMATA in 2026

This is where I need to be completely honest.

Coming into 2026, I expected Resident Evil Requiem to be the Capcom game I would be talking about all year.

Then 007 First Light showed up and reminded everyone how good a proper James Bond game can be.

And now...

PRAGMATA.

Three completely different games.

Three completely different experiences.

And somehow, all three have become essential parts of my 2026 gaming year.

But PRAGMATA hits differently.

Because nobody expected this.

It is not Resident Evil.

It is not Monster Hunter.

It is not Street Fighter.

It is not another established Capcom franchise.

It is new.

And Capcom took a genuine risk.

A big-budget, single-player, linear science-fiction shooter built around a completely new combat mechanic and two characters carrying an emotional story.

In 2026.

That is fucking brave.

Ariya's Final Take

I went into PRAGMATA expecting a cool science-fiction shooter.

I came out of it thinking:

When the hell did Capcom become this good at making me emotionally attached to robots?

PRAGMATA is not perfect.

Its story occasionally relies on familiar ideas.

Some encounters can become overwhelming.

The hacking system may not work equally well for everyone.

But none of that changes what makes this game special.

The shooting is excellent.

The hacking is genuinely inventive.

The art direction is phenomenal.

The level design is focused.

The music and atmosphere are fantastic.

And Hugh and Diana?

Yeah. They got me.

Most importantly, PRAGMATA reminded me why I love games in the first place.

Not because they are massive.

Not because they are photorealistic.

Not because they contain 200 hours of content.

But because somebody can still come up with a strange idea...

and actually fucking commit to it.

Resident Evil Requiem terrified me.

007 First Light made me feel like James Bond.

PRAGMATA made me fall in love with a game I never expected to care this much about.

And that is why...
